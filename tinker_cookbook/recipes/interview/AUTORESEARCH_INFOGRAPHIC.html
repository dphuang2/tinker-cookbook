§ 02

The journey, decomposed.

Five inflection points moved accuracy 16.7 pp in absolute terms. Most of the gain came from a single line of eval config — not from training.

cumulative accuracy on DeepMath 500 ↑ accuracy  ·  → experiment

Three steps were noise-floor improvements. One step — lifting the eval's max_tokens cap from 8192 to 24576 — moved the needle by an order of magnitude more than every prompt-engineering insight combined.

§ 03

Four lessons,
each paid for in commits.

Negative results were the majority of the journey. Each card is the cheapest sentence we could find to convey what 5–15 wasted experiments taught us.

i

SFT actively damages base reasoning.

Every supervised run regressed accuracy. The smallest viable test — twenty records, two gradient steps — was enough to collapse cadence to 99% skip. Prompt-only beats every fine-tuned variant we tried.

0028 · 100 records · 0.482  |  0147 · 20 records · 0.870 @ 99% skip
ii

The eval cap was the hidden bottleneck.

For the first 61 experiments we tuned the prompt against an eval whose max_tokens_per_turn truncated the model's thinking. Lifting it from 8192 to 24576 bought more accuracy than every prompt tweak combined.

0061 · cap 16384 · 0.846  →  0062 · cap 24576 · 0.870  (+9pp over 8192)
iii

Verbose hedging is load-bearing.

“Use three checkpoints” reads as an order. The model takes it literally, tool-spams every problem, and pays the prompt-overhead cost everywhere. “is typical for a multi-step problem” softens the floor and saves the cadence shape.

0128 · terse imperative · 0.828  (−4.5 pp · 91% tool-use)
iv

Two identical samples can happen by chance.

0132 and 0133 both returned 0.890 on the dot. A real signal — until 0134 came in at 0.854. At temperature 0.6, the single-sample standard deviation is around 1 percentage point. Trust three samples, not two.

0132 · 0133 · 0134 → 0.890 / 0.890 / 0.854  ⇒ mean 0.878
§ 04

Cadence, observed.

The model self-organises into two modes: skip the tool entirely on simple problems, or invoke it exactly three times on multi-step ones.

“Three” is the keystone.

Several anchors produced this same shape — but “three” concentrated the right-hand peak most sharply. “Aim for exactly three” overfired (0.828). “A few” smeared the peak across 1 – 4 calls without changing the mean. “Three checkpoints is typical” landed at the sweet spot.

The bimodality is the goal: the model has learnt when to use the tool, not whether to use it. Cheap problems are answered directly; multi-step problems receive three structured progress notes.

The recipe doesn't make the model better at math.
It convinces the model that calling the tool isn't a penalty.
§ 05

The recipe.

Zero training records. Three short blocks of prose. Same eval params as the no-tool baseline. tinker_cookbook/recipes/interview/sft_train.py.

file:sft_train.py commit · current head  ·  0 records 
# All three knobs together. Removing any one of them measurably hurts.

SYSTEM_PROMPT = "You are solving competition math problems. "
                 "You have access to a checkpoint tool for tracking progress."

USER_INSTRUCTION_SUFFIX = (
    " Think step by step, then write your final answer in \\boxed{} format. "
    "Don't think for too long unnecessarily, especially when you have a "
    "reasonable degree of confidence. Use the checkpoint tool when it "
    "helps you organize hard multi-step problems -- three checkpoints "
    "is typical for a multi-step problem."
)

PROGRESS_TOOL_SPEC = {
    "name": "checkpoint",
    "description": "Pause your thinking to record a checkpoint...",
    "parameters": {# single 'message' string, with one u-sub example},
}
